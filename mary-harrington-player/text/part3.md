# The Third Singularity
by Mary Harrington
# Rebuild the Monasteries, part 3

This is part 3 of Rebuild the Monasteries, first delivered at the 2026 Symbolic World conference themed on “Retelling the Cosmic Epic”. My topic is the cultural and spiritual implications of the digital revolution not just for reading, but also human memory.

In Part 1 I described how the “Singularity”, the moment of merger with our digital information technology, has already happened - but wasn’t the first such event. The first was literacy itself, circa 300 AD, which brought a new capacity for abstraction along with a new fear that writing would destroy memory.

Part 2 looked at what actually happened to memory, during the 1,800-odd-year period between literacy and the printing press. In brief: not so much destruction as increasing sophistication, and - especially after the fall of Rome - a convergence of memory technology with Christian spiritual practice, in monastic life. Then I look at what happened when the printing press made the art of memory obsolete.

This is the third and final part. Here we discuss the implications of, perhaps, returning to more medieval levels of general literacy - but, this time, without (at least yet) having established a comparable culture of trained memory. Some of those implications are gloomy - but, as you’ll see, not all of them.

# he Third Singularity
If the form of print turned out to have far-reaching effects on culture and consciousness, the same is happening again now with the digital transition. Why?

Well, Walter Ong predicted that, thanks to TV and radio, we were heading for an age of “secondary orality”: where the spoken word once again took centre-stage. He was right, but ahead of himself; it wasn’t TV and radio that truly turned the tide of mass literacy, in the end. It was smartphones.

For Ivan Illich, the history of the West is the history of our evolving relationship with the alphabet. Well, it just evolved again. Between roughly 2007, when the first iPhone launched, and 2021 when the Covid lockdowns ended, Western (especially Anglophone) cultures transitioned definitively from print-first to digital-first.

Our culture is already as fully merged with this new technology as the premodern one became with print, and prior to that the “Age of Heroes” oral one became with the alphabet. This is true even of those who choose not to have a smartphone: the culture around you is still changed. The medium, as McLuhan famously pointed out, is the message.

In the case of information technologies, the medium also forms human consciousness individually and at scale, in the most direct imaginable fashion. I’m sure you all know the tech-pessimist lament about how scrolling is shredding everyone’s ability to concentrate. I get more book reading done in my annual week camping in a field with no mobile reception, than I do in the months before and after. Digital reading is, by design, a thicket of interruption and distraction.

There are subtler effects, too. Importantly, digital reading is less linear. People skip from text to text, skimming or CTRL+F-ing for relevant keywords. Formally, whereas you have to work quite hard to make a book non-linear in form, you have to work quite hard online to constrain people to a linear text.

The effect is to make digital reading more shallow and patterned. This matters. It creates its own kind of flow state, but this isn’t like the linear print-era form of a sustained argument. The short-form accessibility and high emotional tenor of digital content impairs the ability to focus on cooler, more abstract forms of thought.

What tends to be cultivated instead is associative, pattern-based, imagistic, and often highly poetic in character. But it’s also emotive, drawing sometimes on dark moods. (There’s also a marked correspondence, as I argued here, between standard forms of clickbait, and the medieval Seven Deadly Sins.)

# They Walk Among Us
In this space memetic riptides ebb and flow at speed, sometimes with such force that abstract thought is difficult to sustain. I’m sure you all know the experience of being caught up in an online meme tide. As half a millennium of print culture cracks, taking with it rationalism, objectivity, secularism, materialism, facts, and long-form concentration, so the poetic language of demons, cryptids, possession, and enchantment that characterised premodern cultures have begun to trickle back in.

Another related casualty is the kind of careful fact-checking and verification that became so central a characteristic of the print era, and helped drive its huge advances in knowledge, expertise, and precision. When the British activist Matt Goodwin recently published a polemical book in Britain recently only to be accused of using apparently AI-hallucinated quotes, the scandal blew over in a couple of days while the book stayed on the bestseller list. No one cared, because the arguments felt sufficiently true.

Post-print media dis-incentivises long form, linear reading and deep concentration. It encourages a more participatory, emotion-led relation to the world. It’s less interested in facts, and forms our perception not for abstraction and detachment but pattern recognition. If this is starting to sound a bit like premodern spoken-word culture, that’s no coincidence.

But there’s one crucial difference. We have, as yet, developed no digital-age cultural practices directed at forming human memory. If anything, our culture is still hell-bent on not having to do this.

# Unsouling the West
In their quest to conquer Britain, the Romans realised they needed to obliterate the Celtic people’s sacred tradition. So they set about killing the Druids. Having thus wiped the collective spiritual memory-repository of the Brythonic peoples, they claimed the British Isles for their empire. Temporal domination is downstream of the spiritual kind.

Today a growing subset of the population is now functionally post-literate or even, I suspect in many cases, illiterate. The average adult spends hours a day scrolling, the average number of books read per year is zero, and where people do read books, the prose structure and vocabulary are growing simpler. The topics are more schlocky. The sentences shorter. Educators are everywhere sounding the alarm on a student body that isn’t just unwilling to read but unable.

We should keep this in mind, as we think through the consequences of the age of post-literacy; the machine-scanning of rare books and the shredding of paper copies. It’s one thing to discard the art of memory, because books are so plentiful you no longer need to build a giant, multidimensional mnemonic Ark in your mind. As Maryanne Wolf shows us, within print culture the deep work of contemplation still happened, through the dialogue that occurs for an “expert reader” between the long-form text and other, previous recollected texts. Now, unless we’re deliberate about it, I’m not sure any of this will map onto the digital world.

Even a bookshelf is, in a sense, embodied. I can often remember roughly where something is in a book from tactile data: how thick the wad of pages on each side, or the visual shape of the paragraphs. By contrast, not only is the digital storage of data radically less tactile, the sheer volume of content obliterates any idea we might once have had about knowing “everything”.

The “Renaissance man” was only possible because, at the time, the sum of knowledge was still compact enough to be encompassed by a single person. That’s not been true since perhaps the 19th century and is now palpably, dismayingly false. We can’t know everything, and we know it. So we’re storing it all in computers and hoping for the best. That means we’ve less faith than ever in the value of, and need for, human memory. That in turn is having huge consequences.

# The Great Forgetting
We saw in part 2 how how print rendered the art of memory obsolete. But the formative, ethical dimension of memory didn’t completely go away, even through the print age. Well into my childhood, it was common practice for school children to learn things by rote, on the basis that some stuff is important, to furnish a mind; to think with. But the advent of digitisation ignited a dream: that we could complete the destruction of the memory palaces. Where books partially externalised memory for us, computers could finish the job.

This took shape over the twentieth century, in the “progressive” view of education. Here memory was systematically attacked, on the basis that “critical thinking” and “research skills” are all anyone needs, and kids can Google stuff.

Now LLMs are effectively colonising the whole educational chain. In May last year James D walsh reported that “Everyone is using cheating their way through college” using AI. He cites teachers who report that a majority of their students use AI to complete assignments. Now increasingly tutors are using AI to grade as well.

The common refrain is that the resulting adults will be illiterate. But this isn’t quite right. Outsourcing reading, writing, and memory to AI produces minds that aren’t so much illiterate (they have to write the prompts after all) as unformed. Unaccustomed to thinking without the thought-extending powers of an LLM, and untrained in storing and retrieving anything – even words.

This is not the same thing as being stupid. But you know an un-formed mind when you see it. Such individuals struggle to organise thoughts; their vocabulary is flattened and imprecise; their analysis may be full of unnoticed contradictions. Lest you think it’s just me, in the Financial Times one commentator recently reported on what this looks like in finance interns. The reporter writes: “While those wannabe masters of the universe initially seemed wildly impressive, when senior financiers later probed their ideas they found them alarmingly shallow.”

This will have civilisational-scale repercussions. Unless the robots take over all the jobs, literally all of them, we’re proposing an information society that presupposes objectivity, abstraction, and at least basic intellectual rigour and attention to detail in its functionaries, while at the same time forming people without these characteristics and actively degrading those characteristics in those who were raised with them.

Meanwhile the epistemic environment is being flooded with AI-researched books. Wholly AI-generated research papers. Self-published books, websites, everything. And the LLMs are eating the old books, and spitting out confetti.

Symbolically, the event in Vinge’s story Rainbow’s End is upon us. We’re losing the elaborate, intricate, marvellous culture of mass literacy that formed the backdrop for modernity, along with the faculties it fostered. The lights of mass literacy really are going out, and with them I think high-tech civilisation will end. Likely slowly, as Rome did, but it will end.

As things stand, we have yet to compensate for limited literacy the way the manuscript era did, through the cultivation of memory. Instead, memory too has been outsourced to machines. The data centres springing up across the world are the physical manifestation of our reluctance to take up the challenge of remembering things for ourselves.

# Universal Outsideness
So, then: what happens to memory’s ethical and formative aspect, when we hand both the text and also the practice of memory to a machine? Do we, in the process, risk un-souling ourselves? What will think us, if we relinquish the formation of our own inner realms entirely to algorithms, and the unknown weightings of of LLMs “aligned” to someone else’s vision of the good? Will we even retain a sense of “insideness”?

I don’t think it has to be like that. But inasmuch as we can avert a great un-souling, in the wake of this great forgetting, we need to cast our mind back to Hugh’s Ark. In proper medieval style, we must do so in more than one sense.

If we’re to survive the Great Forgetting, we need to be memorymaxxing. As we saw, for Hugh of St Victor Noah’s Ark served as a model for advanced mnemotechnic practice. Hashtag goals, as the kids say. Build yourself a memory palace. But don’t just do it alone: we should be memorymaxxing together.

This doesn’t have to be religious. Study groups, book clubs, and choirs all do this. Mnemonics. Songs. Memorise the stories of your people: of your family, your town, your tribe, your church. The more you have by heart, the more you can still pass on, after the machines finish eating the books.

It doesn’t have to be religious. But I think we’ll also find that even if we don’t mean for our inner Arks to be religious, they’ll sort of turn out that way anyway. For while at the first level Hugh’s Ark was a memory device, once mastered it was also a multifaceted heuristic for meditation, which is always the telos of learning. The honey of sacred insight distilled: first learned, then applied, and finally internalised, metabolised, embodied.

How, we might wonder, could we ever imagine the memory, considered since ancient times as the seat of the soul and hence of vital ethical importance, could be a target for deliberate formation without this being treated as religious or spiritual? Given this, you can try and be secular but I can almost guarantee it won’t work, and that you might as well treat the formation of memory explicitly as a spiritual path.

Again: this doesn’t have to be Christian. The classical contemplative tradition comprises a cluster of ascetic, disciplinary practices with parallels to the ora et labora of St Benedict – again with the aim of creating space for contemplation. Such praxis is not ordered to instrumental ends, but toward alētheia: contemplative truth, the being of things.

But for those of you who are Christian believers, this is the function of liturgy, especially the structured, formulaic kind. Of daily prayer. What St Benedict called the “work of God”. I don’t mean you necessarily have to pray the Liturgy of the Hours. But I know people who do.

None of this is going to save everyone. But here’s another sense in which Noah’s Ark is relevant. In the Bible story, it was not a universal solution. Most of the world was flooded.

Not everyone longs to create the space and freedom to enable surrender to God in contemplation. Not everyone even feels particularly at home in the world of ideas. But those of us for whom the great forgetting is a tragedy need, urgently, to be building paper libraries – and, also, monasteries of the soul, memory arks. Or, if that’s your calling, monasteries of wood, and stone, and people, books and icons.

# Ascension
In case this seems impossibly gloomy to you, there’s perhaps a note of comfort in sacred time. As the machine eats books and spits out hallucinations, I think we’re going to start seeing secular “history” going with it. That has a great many downsides. But it also re-opens the possibility that, if only we can rebuild our Arks of memory and contemplation, secular time might once again be supplemented the sacred kind, rdered through memory, ritual, and prayer. In this event, we will once again be able to contemplate not just temporal history, but – like Hugh and his Ark – the multidimensional miracle of salvation history.

Here, again, the bees are always at work. The Catholic Easter Vigil makes a special mention of their labour, in the Exultat. The Paschal candle is the work not only of human hands, but also of bees. The bees’ premodern symbolic richness lends a deep resonance to the Paschal sign of sacrifice and resurrection: emblems of memory, meditation, and “holy understanding”. Aided by the bees, then, the Paschal candle images both ora and labora, both prayer and work, sacred and secular together.

In the Western liturgical calendar the feast of Easter, comes to a close with Ascension: the moment the risen Christ returned to heaven. The Easter liturgy gathers Scripture typologically, collapsing events millennia apart into the same salvific story. But the events of the Gospel, and particularly of Ascension, also model this, especially in that moment when Jesus was implored by his disciples to end the pain and exile, and restore Israel.

Jesus, though, replies that no. We can’t have the fulness of sacred time just by wanting it hard enough. “It is not for you to know times or seasons that the Father has fixed by his own authority”. At this climactic moment in salvation history, his instruction to them is: return to Jerusalem, and I will send you the Holy Spirit. The message was clear. Secular time will not be rushed, and salvation history happens to its own temporality, whose authority is God.

When the disciples obeyed, what followed was Pentecost, the beginning of the church history. There, in time, St Augustine and St Benedict founded their orders and with them, the beginning of lectio divina, and its meditative formation. I choose to take from this the thought that yes: the end of mass literacy means the end of secular written memory. With that loss I expect we will lose some or all of the Enlightenment’s cold brightness. There’s much to regret there. But it will also re-open the door to sacred time. And for one thing, that means we can put the End Times back where they belong: not in technological Singularity, but in the sacred time of Revelation.

Meanwhile, as long as we take up the work of bees, the work of memory and contemplation, even if the Enlightenment goes out we can perhaps relight in its place the Paschal candle. And, perhaps, the flame of Pentecost.

With this all in mind, we must follow St Benedict’s example. As those tech-bro entrepreneurs of the memory-eating digital machine are fond of saying: it’s time to build. Literally or figuratively. It’s time to build the monastic, ascetic discipline and contemplative capacity for living, coming together in human memory, that brings together and embodies the word (small w) and the Word (big W) not just in the head, but the heart and the gut. Perhaps for some of us, it’s time to rebuild the actual institutions.

If we manage this, even if Rome falls, as I think it will, a few lamps will go on burning. If that seems cold comfort, perhaps a brighter one is that this kind will re-open the doors again, from secular time and its illusions of Progress, to the sacred time of salvation. Then with our eyes on that horizon, perhaps we can begin in earnest to retell the cosmic epic.
